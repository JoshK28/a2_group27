Acknowledgement of Country

Every AUSSportTix event is played on Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander Country. This page explains what an Acknowledgement of Country is, helping you find whose Country your event is on and shows how to write an acknowledgement that means something.

What is an acknowledgement of Country?

An Acknowledgement of Country is a short statement, given at the start of an event, that recognises the Traditional Custodians of that land that the event is being held on and their continuing connection to it. Anyone may give an acknowledgement.

It matters for reconciliation because it says out loud something Australia ignored for a long time. That this land has been cared for by the First Nations peoples for more than 50,000 years and that connection was never given up. The acknowledgement shows the respect and care that we give for these custodians of this great land that we live upon.

Making it meaningful

There is no set wording for an acknowledgement. These are the recommendations drawn from research. Local advice comes first.

  • Be specific. Name the people and the place. "The turrbal and Jangera peoples" means more than the "Traditional Custodians".
  • Make your acknowledgement your own. Add one setence that only you could write: what playing on this ground means or something your event is doing.
  • Speak in the present tense. Custodians are still here and their connection continues.
  • Keep it short and say it like you mean it. Two to four sentences read at the start of an event, speak with clarity and respect.

Common mistakes and contested issues

  • Copy and pasting an Acknowledgement of Country. The same generic line at every event can feel tokenistic. Use it only if you can't find out whose Country it is.
  • Old or wrong words. "Tribe" and "Aborigines" are outdated; use "people", "nation" or the groups own name.
  • Past Tense. "Who once lived here" erases people who are still here.
  • Calling it a welcome. A Welcome to Country is given by Traditonal Owners or people they authorise; an acnkoledgement doesn't welcome anyone.
  • Guessing from a map. Maps show language areas, ot agreed boundaries. SOme places have more than one group with a connection and some are disputed.
  • "Elders past, present and emerging". Many organisations will now say "past and present", however osme communities prefer otherwise. Check and follow the local advice.
  • Retelling of stories. Dreaming stories and cultural knowledge belong to their people. Link to their own sources instead of retelling them.
